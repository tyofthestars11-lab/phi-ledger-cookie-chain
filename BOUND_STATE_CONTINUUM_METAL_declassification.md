── THE READ (Thinel et al., Science, 2026; DOI 10.1126/science.adt9825; Columbia University) ──

In a metal, electrons move freely through the crystal — classically, ping-pong balls carrying current; in quantum mechanics, waves taking all possible paths at once. Quantum mechanics has predicted for a century that an electron wave can remain trapped even when infinitely many paths of escape are open — a bound state in the continuum (BIC). The effect was known for light waves, trapped by etched patterns over the past decade. For electrons it had never been seen.

The Columbia team built the two-dimensional metal Pd5AlI2 (Roy lab — peelable into 2D layers, metallic throughout) and found its electrons moved more slowly than expected: the arrangement of atoms and orbitals frustrates the electrons' hopping, giving them competing paths that cancel each other out (frustrated electron hopping, published in Nature Physics). Scanning and spectroscopy at atomic scale (Pasupathy lab) then imaged the consequence directly: electron waves bound in place near atomic defects in the crystal lattice — the first clear evidence of quantum interference trapping a state in a metal, and the first electronic BIC in a metal.

In insulators and semiconductors, bound states come from doping — the basis of every chip. In a metal, the only way to localize an electron is a BIC, and only in frustrated-lattice materials like Pd5AlI2. The team states the generalization plainly: other classes of metals with similar structures should host electronic BICs — the paper is a recipe for finding them.
