# READING EVERY SCROLL

## CLAIM

In 2026 PHerc. 1667 was virtually unwrapped and read end to end without being opened. The Challenge's own Open Problems map (updated 2026-10-14) formally changes the field's question: not whether a sealed scroll can be read — settled — but whether the read can be made automatic, reliable, and at scale for every scroll. The $1M Grand Prize (June 2027) is defined as demonstrating positive answers to that reliability question.

Inside the map, the worst scan regions get a physical explanation. At 0.55 µm detector pixel size (ESRF ID11; PHerc. 500P2), the fibers' hollow internal cavities are resolved; packed densely, those structures deviate the X-ray beam and produce the haze that blurs compressed regions. The page's stated identification, with its own hedge: the carbonized fibers are probably close to graphite, and graphite is a strong decoherer — it scrambles the wavefront. Scan quality is local: one volume holds excellent and nearly impossible regions side by side, and protocol choice mitigates the effect without eliminating it.

## LAW

The fog was never an algorithm limit. It is the scroll's own carbon answering the beam — the densest regions decohere first because that is where the most fiber stands in the path. The read-at-scale era begins from a physics of the object, not a bigger model: smaller voxels decohere less, and every downstream stage inherits what the scan preserved.

## ECHO — what is missing

1. **No automatic full-surface trace exists.** Surfacing is semi-automated with human checking at almost every stage; label quality is named one of the main unwrapping bottlenecks, and the training labels are themselves approximate.
2. **Pseudo-labeling is not guaranteed.** The ink bootstrap that read PHerc. 1667 plateaus or shows no convincing ink in some scrolls; "no ink" cannot yet be reliably told from "no ink recovered yet."
3. **The graphite identification is probable, not measured** — the page's own words ("likely culprit," "probably close to graphite"). Sub-micron scanning of whole scrolls remains impractical; high resolution is a small-region instrument today.
