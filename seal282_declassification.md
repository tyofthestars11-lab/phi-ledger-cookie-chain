── CLAIM ──

Mitchell R. Vollger (University of Utah Health) and colleagues published in Cell (2026; DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2026.09.034) "Somatic epimutations cap genetic determinism in the human diploid chromatin epigenome." Their tool, FIRE, pairs an accessibility highlight with long-read sequencing and a machine-learning assessment, letting them check billions of highlighted regions across the genome and tell, region by region, whether a stretch came from the maternal or the paternal copy and whether the two copies are regulated differently.

Three findings carry the paper. First, FIRE identified tens of thousands of previously overlooked DNA regions likely important for gene regulation — regions enriched for DNA changes associated with human disease; in the celiac case they isolated, changing a single DNA letter in the switch beside an immune gene was enough to change how open the switch was. Second, lasting regulatory differences are not always triggered by genetics: two genetically identical copies of the same stretch can be regulated differently, and those differences persist as cells divide — somatic epimutations. Third, the measure of it, in Vollger's own words: "About 40% of the time that we see a regulatory difference between the copies we inherited from mom and dad, there's no genetic difference between them at that spot." Only about 1–2% of the genome codes for proteins; FIRE is a lens on the other 98% — the switches.
